#### both systems: what each does well, and what it doesn't.
I liked how the Spring Spyre was inspired and built upon a previous instrumentation experience (the Lady's Glove), and I think it does a good job of taking ML implementation in separate direction that's less discrete and more focused on the dimensional aspects of ML. It improved on the Lady's Glove in terms of portability and flexibility, as the Lady's Glove was limited by the specific muscle movements in the hand being "tightly mapped to the sound," as Sonami wrote. The instrument itself is designed to, and is highly capable of exhibiting unpredictability due to the Machine Learning interfaces and the physical design itself. While this is something that the Spyre is really good at exhibiting, it can also be a downfall at times. I think this could be the case because unpredictability can make it difficult to achieve a certain sound quickly, and you'll have to experiment a little just to reach the sound you want, if you have a specific one in mind. It also makes the sounds difficult to recreate in general. However, from my understanding, it's the process of figuring out and exploring the sounds that makes it so fascinating to Sonami.

The Sophtar, created by Visi, is an instrument that's a lot more discrete. It does a good job at making it easier to interact with based on human limitations and on what a human is used to. It's built like a tabletop guitar that has pressure sensitive frets and is influenced by different thumb positions. These two parameters seem to make the Machine Learning input aspect easy to interface with I believe. The instrument itself is inspired by the hyperorgan project which allows users to control an organ remotely from anywhere. The Sophtar is similar to this as it allows the user to remote into an instrument via its networking capabilities, typically with a hyperorgan. The machine learning aspects of the instrumentation make it easier for the user to merge and blend sounds together from each of the strings and also adjust cutoff frequencies. Something that I thought was impressive was that the instrument itself does the DSP processing and ML functionally all in its own housing. The creator did mention that it lacks a virtual interface, which I think is something that could be useful for networking/connectivity purposes.

#### A brief compare and contrast. Where do the two disagree about what an instrument should be, if they do?

I found that the biggest differences between the two are in terms of change. Sonami believes an instrument can change and be reflective of the users experiences, and evolve as it performed over long periods of time, while Visi believes instruments, or at least the Sophtar, should be "distinctive, limited, designed, and characterful." Visi wants his instrument to have a distinct identity that remains relatively static, while Sonami wants her instrument to be reflective and dynamic. I did find though that they agreed on not using many GUI/visual interfaces, however Visi employs this for the sake of limiting a user's ability to reprogram and change behavior. I found that they both agreed that their instruments specifically should live more in spaces/dimensions rather than in discrete behaviors.
